# Butwhy

**Find the holes in your plot.** Butwhy reads your outline and asks one question at a time about its weakest spot.

**Try it: https://kejid.github.io/butwhy/**

- Scene 7 just follows scene 6. Did 6 *cause* it, or get in its way?
- The antagonist disappears for the whole middle.
- The ending is decided by luck, not by the hero.
- The twist comes out of nowhere: nothing hints at it earlier.
- The subplot never touches the main plot, so it could be cut without loss.

No AI, no account, no server. It's a single HTML file, and your story stays in your browser.

## How it works

1. **Premise.** One sentence: *who wants what, but what stands in the way, and what is lost if they fail.* The hero can be a person, a group, a place or a thing.
2. **Scenes.** Each scene is linked to the one before it by **therefore** or **but**. “And then” gets flagged: that is where readers start skimming. Each scene makes things better or worse for the hero.
3. **Questions.** The engine checks the structure and asks about the weakest spot first. Answer, skip, or keep writing; it checks again as you go.
4. **Decks for when you're stuck.** Every question comes with the right kind of card: plot moves, character interview prompts, changes of angle, a yes/no oracle. Add your own cards; they come first.
5. **Threads.** Mark subplots and where they meet the main plot, and see the whole story as a metro map.
6. **Plan.** Copy the result as a plain-text outline.

Start with **Paste outline**: one scene per line, and Butwhy builds the chain for you.

## What it checks

Premise and stakes · scenes with no cause (“and then”) · scenes that change nothing · a short or “but”-less middle · nonstop falling or nonstop success · no darkest moment before the ending · who decides the outcome · the ending vs. the last scene · characters with no goal · characters who vanish or appear only at the end · the antagonist's pressure in the middle and presence at the climax · a goal that changes, and whether the ending answers both · secrets with no setup · red herrings with no fair clue · subplots that never meet the main plot or never resolve · theme scenes and epilogues.

The rules are deliberately simple and visible. Butwhy doesn't read your prose; it trusts the links and marks you set. That's the point: it shows you where to look, and the writing stays yours.

## Examples

- **The Hound of the Baskervilles** (Arthur Conan Doyle, public domain), marked up scene by scene, including the red herring, the Barrymore subplot and the moment the goal changes.
- **Pride and Prejudice** (Jane Austen), with the Jane and Bingley subplot, Wickham's lie and Darcy as the obstacle Elizabeth has misjudged.
- **Romeo and Juliet** (William Shakespeare): the feud as a subplot, and the undelivered letter that decides the ending.
- **A Christmas Carol** (Charles Dickens): a middle with not a single “but”, and a goal that changes only at the grave.
- **Frankenstein** (Mary Shelley), with the creature's tale as its own thread and a goal that changes on the glacier.

All four are public domain and marked the way the books are written, not the way the engine would like them.
- **The Lighthouse**, a short original story with holes left in on purpose.

## Run it

Use it online at https://kejid.github.io/butwhy/, or download `index.html` and open it in a browser. That's it.
